In the [previous chapter](/blog/en/migracion-praderas-04-the-agenda-in-your-pocket) the house already answered in your pocket: calendar, tasks, chat. That feeling is powerful. It is also dangerous. Because the next impulse almost writes itself: *if it talks, let it do everything*.

This one is not about more screens. It is about **giving the house hands** — and deciding, calmly, **which ones**.

### The what: a few trades, not a total butler

When an agent stays awake on the server, it does not need to be your second self. It only needs to do **a few jobs well**:

- Stay quiet when things are fine, and **speak only when something breaks**.
- Bring a **short morning briefing** of what is worth reading — not a whole newspaper.
- Move a **card** on the board when you ask in plain language.
- **Publish the blog** when the text is already on the main branch — pull the thread, do not rewrite history.

That is the what. Not “automate life.” Automate **contact with what is yours**, with limits you can say in one sentence.

### The why: the temptation of power without a perimeter

An assistant in someone else’s cloud already comes with a foreign perimeter: their rules, their retention, their product. At home **you** draw the line. That is freedom… and responsibility.

If you give the agent the full server keyboard “because it is easier,” you no longer have a doorkeeper. You have a tenant with a master key. That was never the point of this move. The point was to keep owning the habit: the agenda, the board, the site, the family chat.

So the why is simple: **you want hands, not the remote control for the whole house**. You want the agent to run concrete trades — and if it lies, if it says “done” without touching anything, the failure shows up in *your* thread and can be fixed.

There is a more intimate why too. When family enters the picture, the agent stops being a systems toy. A timely alert, a clean appointment, a “the blog is up” after a merge… are small gestures that hold a shared household together without asking everyone to learn how to deploy.

### The how: split the voices, teach the trades

The how starts by **not mixing**. One room to do things with the cloud and the house. Another to chat and search without touching the server. Same family of agents, different permission. The one on the laptop still holds long context; the one on the server does not sleep.

Then the trades arrive, one by one, the way real trades are taught:

**First, watchfulness that does not shout.** A few scripts check whether the chat bridge still breathes, whether the cloud answers, whether the disk is filling up. If all is well: silence. If not: a message in the automation room. No model reading logs all day — the machine evaluates; the agent, when needed, only helps tell the story.

**Then, contact with content.** Calendar, tasks, board. Not the cloud’s admin panel: what a normal person would want from a phone. Here we learned something that stings and teaches: sometimes the agent **says** it created the appointment and nothing happened. The cure is not to shout louder. It is to demand proof — a clear answer from the tool — and to fix the trade until “created” means created.

**Then the digest.** A short weekday text at a fixed hour, with what matters in your world (self-hosting, tools, privacy). It lands in the same chat. If the prompt leaks into the message, you clean it: the reader only wants the briefing.

**Finally, the blog loop.** Writing and merging stay human work (and review). Publishing can be a house trade: *pull the live tree, forward only, and check that the site answers*. It does not rewrite the past. It does not force. If it cannot go straight, it stops and says so.

None of these steps need a war manual. They need **craftsman’s patience**: one trade, one test, one limit. The model can change; the perimeter stays.

### How it feels when it fits

It is not lab magic. It is more like this: you are on the train, you open the same chat as always, and the house tells you the disk is fine by saying nothing. Or it leaves a card on the board. Or, after you merge an article, you ask it to publish and it returns two 200s and a commit id.

The trick is not the feature list. It is **calibrated trust**: you know what it can do, what it cannot, and where to look when it fails.

### Closing

Talking to the house was the threshold. Giving it hands — few, honest, checkable — is the next stretch of the move. Not to replace you. So that what is yours does not depend on you sitting at a desk.

Mail and other pieces can wait. For now this is enough: **the house answers, watches, briefs, and when it is time, publishes**. You still draw the perimeter.
